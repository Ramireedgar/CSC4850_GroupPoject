That's a great question, and it highlights an important distinction between dataset bias and real-world phenomena!

When we "symmetrized" the dataset in export_scaled_data.py (creating a row from the home team's perspective and another row for the away team's perspective), we removed a structural bias from the dataset.

Without symmetrization, a model might just learn a dumb rule like "always predict a win for team A" because team A in the raw data is always the home team, and home teams win more often. By symmetrizing, we forced the model to learn how to predict a match outcome from both teams' perspectives equally. We balanced the dataset so exactly 50% of the rows have is_home=1 and 50% have is_home=0.

However, symmetrizing the data doesn't erase the real-world home-field advantage.

In professional soccer, playing at home is a massive, statistically significant advantage. Because we kept is_home as a feature (a flag of 1 or 0) in the dataset, the model correctly learned this real-world truth. It figured out that:

If is_home = 1, the probability of match_outcome = 1 (a win) goes up significantly.
If is_home = 0, the probability of a win goes down.
So, why is it a major factor in SHAP? SHAP is just explaining what the model relies on to make decisions. Because the real-world home-field advantage is so strong, is_home is highly predictive of the final score. The model uses it heavily, and SHAP correctly flags it as one of the most important features.

If you truly wanted to force the model to ignore the concept of a home-field advantage (perhaps to evaluate teams purely on their stats in a vacuum), you would need to drop the is_home column entirely before training.

To break it down even further, think about how the raw data was structured before the mirror: Each row was: [Home Team Stats] | [Away Team Stats] | [Match Outcome]

If we trained a model on that raw data, the model wouldn't even need an is_home column. It would quickly figure out a shortcut: "The stats on the left side of the row belong to the team that usually wins." It would bake the home-field advantage into the left-side features implicitly, which is bad if we want a model that evaluates teams fairly.

By mirroring (symmetrizing) the data, we changed it so half the time the stats on the left are the home team, and half the time they are the away team.

This forces the model to:

Actually learn how the performance stats (goals, possession, etc.) interact with winning, regardless of which "side" of the row they are on.
Explicitly rely on the is_home flag to calculate that real-world home-field advantage, rather than relying on the structural bias of the dataset.
And because it now relies heavily on that is_home flag to make accurate predictions, SHAP lights it up as a top feature! You've got it exactly right.
